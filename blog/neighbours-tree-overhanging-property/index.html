Neighbour’s Tree Overhanging Your Property? A South African Guide

Overhanging branches, roots beneath paving and leaves in gutters can be frustrating. A measured, well-documented approach is usually better than reaching for a saw first.

By Nick Tree Felling23 November 20268 min read

If your neighbour’s tree is hanging over your property, start by documenting the problem and speaking to the owner. Do not assume that a branch crossing the boundary automatically gives you the right to remove the whole tree, and do not enter their property or damage roots without appropriate advice.

South African neighbour law tries to balance each owner’s use and enjoyment of their land. In practical terms, that means the severity of the problem, the reasonableness of the proposed solution and the steps already taken all matter.

Important legal note

This article provides general information, not legal advice for your dispute. A South African attorney should review any contested cutting, claim for costs, court process or situation involving significant damage.

Start with these five practical steps

  1. Record what is happening. Take dated photographs of branches, roots, gutters, paving, walls or any other affected area. Keep damaged material or repair reports where relevant.
  2. Identify the immediate concern. Is the issue shade and leaves, physical contact with a roof, visible damage, or a genuine risk of failure? Each calls for a different response.
  3. Speak to the tree owner. Explain the specific problem calmly and show the photographs. They may not be aware of what is happening on your side.
  4. Suggest a practical assessment. A suitable pruning or tree-risk assessment may identify a solution short of removal.
  5. Follow up in writing. Summarise the issue, what was discussed and the action agreed. Written records help prevent misunderstandings.

What does South African neighbour law consider?

The legal discussion cited in this guide explains that property owners have a right to unimpeded enjoyment of their land, but neighbouring owners hold the same right. Where trees interfere with health, wellbeing or comfort, the court may consider whether the interference is objectively unreasonable.

TMJ Attorneys’ overview refers to Malherbe v Ceres Municipality (1951) and explains that, where encroaching branches cause a nuisance, the affected neighbour may request their removal. If the owner fails to act within a reasonable time after demand, remedies may include removing the branches and claiming costs, or seeking an interdict. That is a legal process with facts that matter—not a blanket instruction to cut immediately.

The same source discusses Vogel v Crewe and Another 2003 (4) SA 509 (T), where the court highlighted the value of trees and the need for reasonable tolerance. It also illustrated why evidence matters when alleging that roots or leaves caused particular damage.

Can you cut overhanging branches?

There may be legal remedies for genuine encroachment, but you should not treat the boundary line as permission to do whatever you like. First request that the owner address the problem. Where agreement cannot be reached, obtain legal advice about the next step before cutting.

Even where cutting is ultimately justified, the work should be limited to what is appropriate and carried out without trespassing or creating unnecessary damage. Protected-tree rules and municipal ownership can add separate restrictions.

Also consider the biological effect. Poor pruning can leave a tree unbalanced or vulnerable to decay, potentially making the original safety concern worse. Agree on a competent contractor and a clear scope wherever possible.

What about roots under your paving or wall?

Do not cut roots automatically. Major roots help support and nourish a tree. Severing them can cause decline or instability, creating a hazard that affects both properties.

  • Document the visible damage and its development over time.
  • Consider whether other causes could be involved, such as drainage, construction or soil movement.
  • Arrange an appropriate tree or building assessment where the damage is significant.
  • Discuss root management, barriers, pruning or replacement options with qualified professionals.
  • Get legal guidance before taking unilateral action in a contested situation.

A photograph of lifted paving alone does not always prove the full cause or the best remedy. Evidence collected early can be valuable later.

What not to do during a tree dispute

Do not poison the tree

Deliberately damaging or killing a neighbour’s tree can escalate the dispute and may expose you to legal consequences. Protected species introduce additional offences and licensing issues.

Do not enter the property without permission

A branch crossing a boundary does not give you general permission to access the neighbouring garden, roof or structures.

Do not dump branches back over the fence

Agree on handling and disposal. Throwing debris onto the neighbouring property creates a new problem and makes a cooperative outcome less likely.

Do not remove the whole tree when pruning would solve the issue

South African case law recognises that less drastic solutions may be preferable. Match the remedy to the actual nuisance rather than using a small issue to justify complete removal.

Who pays for the work?

There is no simple answer that applies to every situation. The parties can agree to share costs, one owner may accept responsibility, or a legal remedy may address cost recovery. The legal source above discusses possible recovery after a proper demand and failure to act, but that does not mean every invoice will automatically be recoverable.

Before commissioning work, clarify who is authorising it and how payment will be handled. If costs are likely to become part of a dispute, obtain legal advice first and retain the assessment, correspondence and invoices.

Protected and municipal trees need another check

Neighbour-law considerations do not override protected-tree legislation. A protected tree may require licensing even if its branches cross your boundary. Likewise, a tree on a verge may belong to the municipality rather than the neighbouring homeowner.

What if the tree presents an immediate danger?

Keep people away and do not stand beneath damaged limbs or attempt hazardous cutting. Notify the owner immediately, document the condition from a safe position and contact an appropriate tree professional. Where public land, electricity infrastructure or urgent municipal assistance is involved, contact the responsible authority.

Immediate risk management and the longer-term dispute should be handled as separate issues. Preserve photographs and correspondence once the area is safe.

A simple neighbour-discussion checklist

  • Describe the exact branches, roots or damage—not just “the problem tree”.
  • Share photographs and listen to the owner’s concerns.
  • Discuss whether appropriate pruning could resolve the issue.
  • Agree on access, timing, property protection and debris disposal.
  • Put the agreed scope and cost arrangement in writing.
  • Seek legal advice if agreement cannot be reached or significant damage is claimed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I cut branches from my neighbour’s tree?

Neighbour law can provide remedies where encroaching branches cause a nuisance, but circumstances and process matter. First request action from the owner and obtain legal advice before contested cutting.

Can I cut roots coming under my boundary wall?

Do not cut roots automatically. Root cutting can destabilise or kill a tree and create new risks. Document the problem and obtain arboricultural and legal guidance.

Who pays for removing overhanging branches?

It depends on the agreement and legal circumstances. Do not assume every expense will be recoverable. Discuss payment before work and get legal advice where costs are disputed.

Can I throw cut branches back into my neighbour’s garden?

No. Do not dump branches or debris on another property. Agree on a practical disposal arrangement instead.

Will a court order the whole tree to be removed?

Removal is not automatic. Courts recognise both the impact of problem trees and their value. Pruning or another less drastic solution may be considered where it addresses the nuisance.
