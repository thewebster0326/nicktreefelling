Do You Need Permission to Remove a Tree in Hermanus?

Why ownership alone does not always answer the question—and the checks to make before pruning or felling a tree on private or public land.

By Nick Tree Felling23 November 20268 min read

You do not always need a permit to remove a tree on your own property in Hermanus. But you should never assume that private ownership automatically makes removal permitted. The species, exact location and property-specific rules can all change the answer.

The safest approach is to identify the tree, confirm whether it is private or municipal, and check for national, municipal, heritage, planning and estate restrictions before any cutting begins.

Quick decision guide

  • Unprotected tree entirely on your private property: removal is often allowed, provided no other restriction applies.
  • Tree on a pavement, verge or public open space: apply to the municipality before any pruning or removal.
  • Protected species or protected tree: national licensing rules may apply, even on private land.
  • Building, estate or heritage context: additional approvals may be needed.

Can you remove a tree on your own property?

In many ordinary cases, an owner can remove an unprotected tree growing entirely within a private property without applying for a national tree permit. That is the starting point—not the end of the check.

You may still need permission or another form of approval where the tree is protected, affected by an approved development, covered by a heritage rule, or restricted by title deeds, estate rules or planning conditions. Heavy pruning can also be treated differently from a light trim when it damages or destroys a protected tree.

If the trunk stands on a boundary or the tree belongs to a neighbour, ownership and neighbour-law questions arise. Our guide to overhanging branches and encroaching roots covers that situation separately.

Trees on pavements, verges and public land

A tree directly outside your wall may look like part of your garden while actually standing on municipal property. Overstrand Municipality states that pruning or removing trees on sidewalks and public open spaces without permission is illegal, whether the trees are indigenous or exotic.

Residents who want a public tree pruned or removed must complete the municipality’s application process. The municipality assesses each request and makes the decision. It generally considers removal only where justified—for example, where a tree is dead or threatens life or property—and does not prune or top trees merely to improve a view.

Do not poison, cut or arrange the removal of a municipal tree yourself. Start with the official application and allow the municipality to assess it.

Protected trees under South African law

The National Forests Act protects listed tree species and certain individually declared trees. Section 15 of the Act restricts cutting, disturbing, damaging or destroying protected trees without the required licence. Protection can apply on private property and can include dead protected trees.

White milkwood (Sideroxylon inerme) is a familiar example in the Overstrand. The municipality’s fire-wise policy discusses limited pruning under an exemption, including a maximum of 25% in qualifying circumstances, and makes clear that topping is not permitted. Natural forest and other protected contexts need particular care.

Other well-known protected South African trees include yellowwoods, stinkwood and baobab, but the official list is much broader and can be updated. Use the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE) resources rather than relying on a short internet list.

Heritage, development and estate rules

Heritage or conservation context

A property’s location can introduce controls beyond the tree species itself. Heritage areas, environmental overlays and conservation-sensitive sites may require additional review. Check with the municipality before removing mature or significant vegetation.

Building and development plans

Where a tree is affected by a new building, extension, driveway or other development, it may form part of the planning assessment. Failing to declare or protect trees required by an approval can delay a project or create compliance problems.

Title deeds and estate rules

Homeowners’ associations, body-corporate rules and title-deed conditions can restrict tree work even where national law does not require a permit. Review the documents that apply to your property and ask the relevant managing body when needed.

Do you need permission to prune rather than remove?

Pruning an unprotected private tree is generally less restricted than removing it, but the same location and ownership checks still matter. Municipal trees require the municipal process, and protected trees have specific limits and exclusions.

“Pruning” should not become a workaround for destroying a protected tree. Topping, severe crown reduction or repeated cutting that kills the tree can have the same practical outcome as removal. If the tree is simply too large for its position, get advice on lawful, biologically appropriate options.

Five checks before you arrange tree removal

  1. Identify the exact location. Is the trunk entirely on your property, on a boundary, or on a municipal verge?
  2. Identify the species. Take clear photographs of the whole tree, leaves, bark, flowers or fruit. Get help where identification is uncertain.
  3. Check the official protected-tree list. Use current DFFE publications and ask about licensing where necessary.
  4. Check local and property documents. Consider municipal rules, heritage or environmental overlays, estate rules, title deeds and development approvals.
  5. Keep records. Retain applications, approvals, photographs and correspondence before scheduling the work.

What if a tree is immediately dangerous?

Keep people away from the hazard and do not attempt dangerous cutting yourself. Photograph the condition if it is safe to do so and contact the appropriate authority and a professional tree service. Where a municipal tree is involved, notify the municipality through its official channels.

An urgent situation does not create a universal rule that every protected tree can simply be removed without process. The response should address immediate safety while preserving evidence and obtaining the necessary direction as quickly as possible.

Where to ask for help

For municipal trees and local requirements, start with Overstrand Municipality. For protected-tree licensing, consult the DFFE’s official forms and publications. Property documents, estate management and planning professionals may also be relevant.

Frequently asked questions

Can I cut down a tree on my own property in Hermanus?

Often you can if it is unprotected and no other restriction applies. Protected species, heritage or planning conditions, estate rules, title deeds and development approvals can change that, so check first.

Can I remove a tree on the pavement outside my house?

Not without permission. It may be on municipal land even when directly outside your boundary. Overstrand Municipality requires an application for pruning or removal of public trees.

Are milkwood trees protected?

Yes. White milkwood is protected under the National Forests Act. Limited pruning may be allowed under an exemption in some circumstances, but topping and unpermitted destruction are not allowed.

Do I need a permit to trim a tree?

Light lawful pruning of an unprotected private tree is different from removal, but protected and municipal trees have additional rules. Excessive pruning can also damage or destroy a protected tree.

Who should I contact before removing a tree in Hermanus?

Start with Overstrand Municipality for local requirements and DFFE for protected-tree licensing. Also check your estate rules, title deeds and planning approvals.
