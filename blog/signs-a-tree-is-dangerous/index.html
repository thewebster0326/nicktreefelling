9 Warning Signs a Tree May Be Dangerous—and What to Do Next

A tree does not need to be dead to be dangerous. Learn which visible changes deserve a professional assessment and when to keep people away immediately.

By Nick Tree Felling23 November 20269 min read

Warning signs of a dangerous tree include a sudden lean, a major trunk crack, hanging branches, root movement and dead limbs above places people use. These signs do not prove the tree must be removed, but they do mean you should stop and have the risk assessed rather than ignore it.

A dangerous tree is about both the chance of failure and what it could hit. A defective branch over an empty field does not present the same risk as one above a bedroom, driveway, playground or public path.

Keep your distance first

If a tree or limb is moving, cracking, leaning suddenly or contacting an electrical line, keep everyone away. Do not stand beneath it, climb it or try to pull a hanging branch down. Contact the appropriate emergency or utility service where necessary.

1. A new or suddenly increasing lean

Some trees grow with a stable lean for years. A new lean, a lean that is getting worse, or movement in the soil on the opposite side is different. It may indicate root or structural failure.

Photograph the tree from a safe position and note when the change was first noticed. Keep people and vehicles away from the potential fall area until the tree has been assessed.

2. Cracks or splits in the trunk or major limbs

A deep vertical split, a widening crack or a major limb beginning to separate can signal serious structural weakness. Included bark at a poorly formed union may also be associated with weakness, but appearance alone does not tell you how imminent failure is.

Do not drill, screw, strap or cut the union yourself. The right response may involve pruning, support, monitoring or removal depending on the defect and target.

3. Broken or hanging branches

A branch that is caught in the canopy can fall without warning, especially in wind. It may look securely lodged until it moves. Do not stand underneath it or throw objects to dislodge it.

Mark off the area below if you can do so without entering the danger zone and arrange professional removal of the hazard.

4. Lifted soil, exposed roots or root damage

Heaving soil, a newly exposed root plate, major severed roots or recent excavation beside a tree can undermine stability. Root damage is not always visible above ground, so nearby construction history matters.

Do not cut large roots to solve paving or wall damage without advice. That can turn a manageable tree into an unstable one.

5. Large dead branches or extensive deadwood

Dead branches can become brittle and fall, but a single small dead twig does not mean the whole tree is failing. Look at the size, location and amount of deadwood, and whether it sits above people or structures.

Repeated dead branches in one part of the crown may also point to a broader health or structural issue that needs investigation.

6. Cavities, decay or fungal growth

Cavities and fungal fruiting bodies can indicate decay, but they do not provide a simple percentage of strength loss. A tree can look solid outside while internal wood is compromised.

Photograph the location and arrange an assessment rather than pushing tools into the cavity or cutting away decayed wood yourself.

7. A major change in the crown

Sudden thinning, unusual leaf loss, a dying crown or one side failing to produce normal growth can signal a health or root problem. Seasonal leaf drop is normal for many deciduous trees, so compare the change with the species and time of year.

A declining crown may require health care rather than immediate felling, but the risk of falling dead material should still be considered.

8. Contact with roofs, walls or power lines

Branches rubbing a roof or wall can cause damage and may be prunable. Electrical contact is far more serious: keep well away and contact the electricity authority. Do not assume a branch is safe because it is not sparking.

9. New damage after a storm

After strong wind or heavy rain, inspect from a safe distance for fresh cracks, broken limbs, shifted soil and changes in lean. Saturated ground can reduce root support, while wind can load weak branches in ways they have not experienced before.

Do not wait for another storm to test whether a damaged tree will hold. A timely assessment may allow selective pruning rather than complete removal.

What to do next

  1. Keep people and pets away from the suspected fall area.
  2. Photograph from safety. Include the whole tree, the defect and nearby targets.
  3. Record changes. Note storms, construction, root cutting and when symptoms appeared.
  4. Check utilities. Contact the electricity authority for electrical contact or suspected contact.
  5. Arrange an assessment. Ask whether monitoring, pruning, support or removal is most appropriate.
  6. Check permissions. Protected or municipal trees may require additional steps even when work is necessary.

Frequently asked questions

Does a leaning tree always need to be removed?

No. Some leans are stable, but a new or increasing lean needs prompt assessment, especially with soil movement or root damage. Removal is not the only possible response.

Are dead branches dangerous?

They can be, particularly when large or above people and structures. The size, condition and target determine the urgency.

Does fungus on a trunk mean the tree will fall?

Not necessarily, but fungal growth can indicate decay and should be assessed. Do not rely on appearance alone to judge structural strength.

Can a storm-damaged tree be saved?

Sometimes. Suitable pruning or monitoring may be enough where the structure remains sound. Major root, trunk or union failure can require a different response.

Who should I call for a tree touching a power line?

Keep away and contact the relevant electricity authority or emergency service. Do not approach or attempt to trim the branch yourself.
